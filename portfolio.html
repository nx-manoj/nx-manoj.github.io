About

I care more about the cleaning than the model

Most of the value in a data project is decided before any model is fitted. In the Bengaluru study, one row in seven was unusable — duplicates, impossible floor areas, area stored as text across six units. In the air-quality study, the monitoring network grew twelve-fold, so a national average would have measured coverage rather than air. Both of those are data decisions, and both change the answer.

So I work in a fixed order: audit the raw file, make every exclusion explicit and counted, model against a naive baseline, then report the failure modes. A model that under-predicts above ₹1.5 crore is more useful described than hidden.

Each project ships as a reproducible notebook plus a written report and a deck — the same finding in three formats, all generated from one source so no number drifts between them.

Case study · Regression · 13,320 listings

What actually sets a Bengaluru asking price

Built-up area explains most of the price and locality sets the rate — the same square foot costs 4.8× more in one part of the city than another. A gradient-boosted model prices a held-out listing to a 17.7% median error, and under-predicts above ₹1.5 crore.

Data

13,320 Bengaluru property listings, 9 columns; 11,423 usable after cleaning.

Methods

Five models on 5-fold CV, log-price target, one held-out fifth, permutation importance.

Deliverables

Executed notebook, six-page report, eleven-slide deck.

The question

Can a listing's price be predicted well enough to be useful?

A buyer in Bengaluru is quoted ₹1.2 crore for a 3BHK. A seller in Jayanagar is told their flat is worth ₹90 lakh. Neither number arrives with an explanation. The question was whether the asking price can be decomposed — what part is area, what part is location, what part is simply the configuration — and whether a model could serve as a sanity check on a quote.

What I did

Every exclusion is explicit and counted

The raw file stores built-up area as text, including ranges such as 1000 - 1500 and six different units (square metres, yards, acres, cents, guntha, perch). Parsing that was a real step, not a formality. Cleaning then applied five rules — plausibility of area per bedroom, bathroom counts, price per square foot against locality norms, exact duplicates — and one row in seven was discarded.

  • Leakage prevented by construction: imputation, scaling and encoding all sit inside a scikit-learn pipeline, so no fold sees the validation data.
  • Target transformed: prices span ₹8 lakh to ₹3,600 lakh, so the model works in log-price and errors are reported back in rupees.
  • Baseline first: a median-price guess costs ₹56.0 lakh per listing; anything worse than that is decoration.

What the data showed

01

Locality is worth 4.8×

The same square foot costs ₹2,885 in Attibele and ₹13,719 in Rajaji Nagar. No other variable in the data spans that range.

02

A 3BHK costs more than its extra floor

Across 156 localities, a 3BHK asks 51% more than a 2BHK while offering 37% more area — about 10% of the jump buys configuration, not square feet.

03

Area type is overrated

Carpet area carries only a 14% premium per square foot over super built-up — roughly the loading factor itself.

04

The model beats the baseline by half

Held-out error of ₹27.6 lakh against ₹56.0 lakh for a median guess; 55% of listings land within 20% of their actual price.

A model that is unbiased in the middle and wrong at the top is a checking tool, not a valuation engine.

Where it stops

Asking prices, one snapshot, no builder signal

These are listed prices, not transactions — in India the two differ, and the model estimates the quote rather than the cheque. The data is a single snapshot with no time dimension, so it cannot capture the market cycle. Society is 41% missing, which removes the strongest quality variable a human valuer would use. And above ₹150 lakh the model under-predicts by design: tree ensembles cannot extrapolate past their training range, and luxury listings are rare and heterogeneous.

Case study · Time series & forecasting · 2017–2023

Seven years of Indian air, on a like-for-like panel

The monitoring network grew twelve-fold between 2017 and 2023, so a national average over all available rows measures coverage rather than air. Rebuilt on a constant panel, mean AQI fell 17.9% — while the seasonal cycle moves 2.6× more than that.

Data

562,878 CPCB station-days, collapsed to 254,138 city-days across 248 cities.

Methods

Constant panels, monthly and hourly decomposition, four forecast candidates with a time-based split.

Deliverables

Executed notebook, seven-page report, thirteen-slide deck in two designs.

The question

Has Indian air actually improved?

India publishes more air-quality data than almost any country and reads very little of it carefully. Two failure modes are common: quoting a national average across a network that is growing, and treating a seasonal spike as a trend. The question was whether air quality improved between 2017 and 2023 — and how much of any apparent change is real.

The trap

The network grew twelve-fold, which fakes a trend

City-days reported rise from 5,575 in 2017 to 69,533 in 2023 as the network expanded from 48 cities to 247. Any average over all available rows mixes real change with who happened to be measuring. Every trend in the study therefore uses a constant panel: cities reporting at least 180 days in every year. A second, denser panel of 52 cities over 2019–2023 is used as a robustness check, and agrees on the direction.

What the data showed

01

Air quality improved, modestly

On 12 cities with data in all seven years, mean AQI fell from 169 to 138 — down 17.9%. Nine of the twelve improved.

02

Season dwarfs trend

November runs 177 against July's 69 — a 2.56× swing inside one year, against an 17.9% movement across seven.

03

The daily cycle is physics, not traffic

AQI bottoms out at 17:00 and peaks at 22:00, a 41% swing. Afternoon mixing disperses pollutants; the evening inversion traps them.

04

Forecasting barely beats persistence

A ridge model improves on "tomorrow = today" by 8.8% MAE. Gradient boosting adds nothing — the ceiling is missing information, not capacity.

A November-to-November comparison is the only one that separates policy from meteorology.

Where it stops

AQI is an index, not a measurement

AQI reports the worst sub-index across pollutants, so it hides which pollutant drove a given day — and this dataset carries no pollutant breakdown. One number per city per day erases the intra-urban variation that determines personal exposure. Station siting changes produce step changes that look exactly like trends. And with no meteorology in the model, the forecast ceiling stays low.
